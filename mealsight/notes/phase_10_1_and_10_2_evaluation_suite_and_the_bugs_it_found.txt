PHASE 10.1 + 10.2 STUDY NOTES: BUILDING A FIVE-DIMENSION EVALUATION SUITE
FOR AN LLM-POWERED MEAL RECOMMENDER, AND THE SIXTEEN-PLUS REAL BUGS IT
FOUND IN CODE THAT ALREADY HAD PASSING UNIT TESTS
=====================================================================

AUDIENCE AND PURPOSE. These notes are written for me, for interview
preparation. Recruiters and interviewers will probe this phase hardest,
because it is the part of the project that shows engineering judgment
rather than feature-building. Every section is written so I can lift a
story, a number or a decision straight out of it. Section 0 is the
30-second and 2-minute pitch. Sections 1-3 are what exists and why.
Section 4 is the chronological story, including everything that failed.
Section 5 is the bug catalogue. Section 6 is my own mistakes (the most
persuasive part in an interview, if told honestly). Section 7 is the
principles. Section 8 is a numbers cheat sheet. Section 9 is likely
interview questions with answers. Section 10 is what NOT to overclaim.

Companion documents: docs/EVALUATION.md (the standing record of the
final result), backend/eval/README.md (the running log of every run and
correction), backend/eval/results/*.json (every scorecard).


---------------------------------------------------------------------
0. THE PITCH
---------------------------------------------------------------------

THIRTY SECONDS.
"MealSight is a multimodal meal recommender: a photo of your fridge, a
voice memo or typed text goes in, and an agent pipeline over three MCP
servers recommends something you can actually cook. In phases 10.1 and
10.2 I built a five-dimension evaluation suite for it, 150 cases, and
used it to find and fix real defects. It found about sixteen, several of
them safety-relevant: a saved nut allergy that was stored correctly and
never applied, avoid-lists that only existed as prompt text, and
cuisine-preference learning that had never worked outside tests. Every
one of those had passing unit tests, because the code was correct and
was simply never called correctly."

TWO MINUTES (add this):
"The interesting part was making the evaluation trustworthy. I derived
the expected answers by hand before running the system, so the tests
could not be tautological. I separated 'the system failed' from 'my test
case was badly designed' and reported both honestly. I ran mutation
tests on my own test cases and found one that could not fail, and
replaced it. I isolated the stateful dimensions onto throwaway databases
and fingerprinted the real ones to prove they were never touched. And I
never lowered a threshold: the suite's final verdict is FAIL, because
ingredient recognition from photos scores 0.49 against 0.60 after the
original vision model was retired and the replacement model tier is the
only one reachable on the free plan. I would rather ship a suite that
says FAIL and explains why than one that says PASS."

ONE-LINE TAKEAWAY: "I built the measurement first, trusted it only after
attacking it, and let it tell me where the product was wrong."


---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT THE SYSTEM IS (SO THE EVALUATION MAKES SENSE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Pipeline (LangGraph, 11 nodes): validate_input -> perceive (vision /
audio / text, via Mistral) -> merge (fusion of the modalities plus the
saved user profile) -> update_pantry -> get_context -> search_recipes ->
match_rank -> reason (an LLM chooses one recipe from a shortlist and
explains) -> generate_output -> record_outcome -> present.

Three MCP servers (FastMCP, stdio subprocesses): recipe_engine (search,
ingredient matching, scaling, nutrition, substitutions), pantry_manager
(pantry, expiry, grocery lists, waste), user_intelligence (profile,
meal history, preference learning, repetition, context).

Data: SQLite, three databases. 250 recipes (TheMealDB-derived), a
147-row synonym table (now 154), a 57-row substitution table, a
nutrition reference table. Models: ministral-8b-2512 for vision,
reasoning and extraction (the free-tier-reachable replacement for a
retired model).

Why this matters for the evaluation: the system is a mix of
DETERMINISTIC code (matching, ranking, filtering, scoring) and an LLM
(perception and the final choice). A good evaluation has to test both
and, crucially, tell them apart when something goes wrong. A large part
of what I found was a deterministic layer being correct but fed wrong
inputs, or the LLM overriding a correct deterministic ranking.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE FIVE DIMENSIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------

D1 INGREDIENT RECOGNITION (30 photos, F1, threshold 0.60).
   From a photo, does vision name what is there? Hand-labelled ground
   truth, a deliberately strict matcher (scallion != onion; sweet
   potato != potato), visibility-adjusted recall. FINAL: 0.488 FAIL.

D2 CONSTRAINT SATISFACTION (55 cases; hard 1.0, soft 0.5).
   Does the system ever break what the user asked? Dietary restrictions
   (alone and stacked), cook time, servings, avoid-ingredient and
   avoid-dish lists, cuisine preference, and requests that should be
   honestly refused. Hard constraints are binary, no partial credit,
   because a dietary violation is a real harm and not a quality miss. A
   satisfiable request wrongly refused FAILS; a correct refusal PASSES.
   FINAL: 55/55, 0 violations. Soft cuisine 1.000 over the 9 cases where
   a hit was possible (0.60 pooled over all 15 cuisine cases).

D3 MATCH ACCURACY (32 hand-derived cases, threshold 1.0, no API calls).
   When the matcher reports a score, is the arithmetic right? Scores
   TWO things separately: the numeric score and the per-ingredient
   classification (matched / substitutable / partial / missing). A case
   where the score is right but two ingredients are misclassified FAILS.
   FINAL: 32/32.

D4 FRESHNESS PRIORITIZATION (15 cases, threshold 0.80).
   When something is near expiry, does the recommendation use it? FINAL:
   14/15 (the one failure, d4_13, is the LLM overriding a correct
   ranking).

D5 PERSONALIZATION (18 cases, threshold 0.80).
   After seeding a profile, do recommendations align with it? Cuisine
   learned from ratings, disliked ingredients, protein preference,
   household size, cook time as a soft default, repetition avoidance,
   saved dietary restrictions. FINAL: 18/18.

COMBINED FINAL (run_1794032750): 150 cases, 118 model calls, 425,642
tokens, 680 s. Overall FAIL because D1 fails. Four of five dimensions
pass.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE METHODOLOGY (THE PART THAT MAKES IT CREDIBLE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 HAND-DERIVED EXPECTATIONS, NOT GENERATED BY RUNNING THE SYSTEM.
If you compute the expected answer by running the code under test, the
test is a tautology: it can only ever confirm the code agrees with
itself. For D3 I worked every expected classification and score out by
hand from the real recipe rows, the full synonym and substitution
tables, and the matcher's documented rules, BEFORE running it. I
recorded a hash of the case file before the first run, and wrote a test
that applies the documented formula (not the matcher) to each case's own
expected buckets to catch my arithmetic slips.
   Interview line: "A test whose oracle is the implementation can't
   fail. So I made the oracle independent: hand derivation for the
   matcher, and a separate literal copy of the ranking weights for the
   ranking checks."

3.2 SYSTEM FAILURE vs CASE DEFECT.
Each case states the situation it claims to create. The harness
independently computes the situation the pantry actually produces. If
they differ, the case is reported as a CASE DEFECT, never counted as a
system failure. This caught real mistakes of mine (section 6).

3.3 FAIRNESS: JUDGE AGAINST WHAT WAS POSSIBLE.
Before judging what the system did, the suite computes what it COULD
have done: it runs the matcher over all 250 recipes for the case's
pantry to find the cookable set. An expiring ingredient no cookable
recipe uses is not a failure. A preferred cuisine nothing cookable
belongs to is not a failure. A far better recipe beating a marginal one
that uses the expiring item is CORRECT deprioritization (the freshness
bonus is 0.15 against 0.60 for ingredient match, so a match advantage
over 0.25 outweighs it). Only "a cookable option honouring the signal
existed, and the system neither used it nor was correctly out-scored"
fails.

3.4 ISOLATION FOR STATEFUL DIMENSIONS.
D4 and D5 must put the system in an exact state per case (a pantry with
one item expiring tomorrow; a profile with ten five-star Italian
ratings). Doing that to the real databases would destroy the seeded demo
pantry and real history. So each case runs against throwaway SQLite
files in a temp directory; the three MCP servers are pointed at them via
a new MCPClientManager(env=...) option. Between cases the files are
deleted and recreated from the real schema and the row count of every
table is ASSERTED zero (the reset raises otherwise). Seeded state is read
back through the servers' own read functions and compared with what the
case asked for. The real pantry/user/recipe databases are content-hashed
before and after each dimension and must be unchanged.

3.5 MUTATION TESTING MY OWN TEST CASES.
Passing is cheap. To check the D3 cases really exercise the paths they
claim, I mutated the matcher 13 ways (no protected terms, substitute
weight 1.0, ignore dietary restrictions on substitutes, no critical
penalty, longer expiry grace, a higher threshold, no specificity
matching, no synonyms, avoid filter off, can_cook ignoring critical,
...) and required each mutation to make cases fail. One did not:
removing the entire protected-term list changed nothing. That exposed a
weak case (details in section 4.8).

3.6 PRE-REGISTERED THRESHOLDS, NEVER ADJUSTED.
Thresholds were fixed before the first run of each dimension: 1.0 for D2
hard and D3, 0.5 for soft cuisine, 0.60 for D1, 0.80 for D4 and D5. I
did not change any to make a result pass. D1 fails and the document says
so.

3.7 FULL PROVENANCE ON EVERY SCORECARD.
Model ids; hashes of the three prompts (vision, reasoning, extraction);
hashes of lexicons and rule sets in code (diet groups, protein lexicon,
cuisine families, avoid groups, restriction patterns, matcher
normalization, thresholds, ranking weights); hashes of reference data
read at run time (recipe corpus, synonym, substitution, nutrition
tables); the pantry fixture and a content hash; git commit, dirty flag
and a diff hash. KNOWN LIMIT: the component hashes cover lexicons,
prompts and data, not code LOGIC; a change in how match_rank uses a
lexicon moves only the git diff hash.

3.8 REPORTING DISCIPLINE.
Every disagreement reported in full with my judgment of which side is
right. Re-baselines labelled as such. Corrections recorded in the case
files with the original values kept. Wall-clock reported as measured,
not retried until fast.

4. THE CHRONOLOGICAL STORY (INCLUDING EVERYTHING THAT FAILED)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

STAGE 1: GROUNDWORK (FIXES THAT MADE THE EVALUATION POSSIBLE)
-----------------------------------------------------------
Before an evaluation means anything the system under test has to be in a
known state. Early work in this phase:

* Long-expired pantry items were still being offered to the matcher. An
  item far past its shelf life is waste, not an ingredient. Fixed: items
  more than expired_grace_days (2) past shelf life are excluded from
  matching entirely, through one shared cutoff function used by both the
  freshness bonus and the matcher so the two can never disagree about
  whether something is food or waste.

* Pantry freshness decay bug. Re-photographing an item reset its clock
  in a way that made fresh items look old (or vice versa). Fix:
  added_date is KEPT on re-sighting; it resets only if the row's age has
  reached the shelf life AND the photo explicitly observed "fresh". An
  unobserved default of "fresh" is NOT evidence. I tested a re-
  photographed item becoming fresh, and wrote a factual verifier that
  checks the system's explanation claims against the data (0 discrepancies
  after the fix).

* Seeded a REPRODUCIBLE demo pantry. A script
  (scripts/reset_demo_pantry.py) rebuilds it from a fixture (test_data/
  demo_pantry.json): the raw vision output of 6 real photos plus 14
  hand-added staples (oil, salt, pepper, flour, sugar, common spices; no
  eggs, rice, pasta or proteins). It makes no API calls, refuses to run if
  the servers are up, backs up the old database with a checksum, and
  collapses duplicates. Result: 64 rows; 22 of 250 recipes cookable; mean
  match 0.195 (before: 4 cookable, mean 0.050). Fixture hash 756090ab287f.

* Protein preference ranking was missing. A user asking for "salmon" or
  "something with shrimp" got no ranking lift. I diagnosed it BEFORE
  fixing it and reported the diagnosis. Fix: a shared protein lexicon
  (fish, shellfish, seafood, poultry, red meat, "meat"), whole-word
  matching (so "egg" never matches "eggplant"), flavouring words
  (stock, broth, sauce, paste, oil...) excluded because a vegetable soup
  with chicken stock is not a chicken dish; a ranking term of 0.15 that
  applies ONLY to recipes that have the protein AND are cookable, so an
  uncookable seafood dish can never outrank a cookable chicken one; and
  an honest explanation ("your pantry has no X") when it cannot be met.
  A related decoupling fix: fusion used to add a WANTED protein to the
  pantry as if it were possessed, so "anything with chicken" made every
  chicken recipe cookable. Removed.

* Restored the spoken-ingredient path properly. The extraction model now
  distinguishes WANT ("I want salmon") from HAVE ("I have salmon"); only
  "have" reaches matching, flagged unverified. Measured before keeping:
  0 wrong of 60 possession probes; extraction accuracy 69/70 text and
  137/140 voice with 0 polarity inversions.

* Prompt comparison, not guessing. Three vision-prompt variants on the
  replacement model, photos 1-5, 3 reps, temperature 0: A 0.478 +/- 0.083,
  B 0.540 +/- 0.052, C 0.536 +/- 0.069. B was kept because no variant
  measurably beat it. Rule I set in advance: adopt another only if it
  measurably beats B; no wording iteration.

* Verified the scorers themselves. 48 known-answer fixtures (a dietary
  violation must fail; a correct refusal must pass; and so on) and 12
  defects in my own scorers fixed, including: a photo where the model
  identified nothing used to be silently DROPPED from the mean (now a
  zero, which can only lower the score); "green onion" matching "onion";
  a Python 3.11 argparse crash on an empty positional list.

D1 re-baseline: F1 0.485 (precision 0.538, recall 0.443), recorded as
superseding earlier figures that used a more lenient scorer.


STAGE 2: DIMENSION 2, FIRST FULL RUN: 20/30, ZERO VIOLATIONS, TEN REFUSALS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Result: not a single dietary violation (good), but 10 satisfiable
requests were wrongly refused (bad). All 10 failures were refusals, not
violations. I diagnosed before changing anything and found three
independent causes.

CAUSE A: A PROFILE DEFAULT WAS ENFORCED AS A HARD CAP.
The profile's preferred_cook_time_minutes (30) is not something the user
ever set; it is DEFAULT_PROFILE_VALUES. Fusion copied it into the
request, and search applied it as a SQL filter. Boulangere Potatoes (60
min) satisfied vegan + gluten-free + dairy-free + nut-free and was
cookable, and it was excluded by a limit the user never stated.
   Fix: provenance. UnifiedMealRequest.max_cook_time_is_default.
   Only a STATED limit filters search; a defaulted one only nudges
   ranking and is described to the model as "a default, not a limit".

CAUSE B: RELAXATION KEYED ON "EMPTY", NOT ON "NOTHING COOKABLE".
search_recipes relaxed its filters (meal-type guess, cook time, cuisine)
only when it found zero recipes. For "Italian vegetarian dinner" it found
three Italian vegetarian recipes, none cookable from the pantry, so it
never relaxed, and the reasoning layer refused. To the user, a non-empty
set of uncookable recipes is the same as no answer.
   Fix: after searching, probe whether any candidate is actually cookable
   (and respects avoid lists and restrictions); if not, loosen the meal-
   type guess then the soft cuisine preference. Dietary restrictions and
   a user-stated cook time never loosen on that path.

CAUSE C: THREE OF MY OWN EXPECTATIONS WERE WRONG.
d2_10 ("vegan, gluten-free, nut-free") I had authored as unsatisfiable;
Boulangere satisfies all three. It had "passed" as a correct refusal for
the WRONG reason (the 30-minute default excluded it). d2_12 (15 minutes):
no cookable recipe is under 20 minutes, so the refusal was correct and my
expectation was wrong. d2_30: the only vegan + dairy-free recipe takes 60
minutes against a stated 30. I corrected all three in the case file with
dated notes and kept the original values; the first run's scorecard keeps
the originals.
   Honest caveat I carry forever: this makes D2's move from 20/30 to
   55/55 a post-hoc re-baseline, not a blind result.

Then the nudge. With the hard cap removed, plain requests like "a recipe
for 4 people" returned a 60-minute recipe for a user whose preference is
30. I had set the ranking nudge to 0.05 without measuring. I swept 0.0,
0.05, 0.15, 0.25 over 14 no-time requests with a SELECTION RULE FIXED
BEFORE RUNNING (smallest weight at which at least 80% of top-ranked
recipes fall within the preference, with no more than 0.25 match score
given up and every pick cookable). Two things went wrong and are worth
telling:
   (1) BUG IN MY OWN EARLIER CHANGE: the repetition re-score of the top
       candidates rebuilt the composite WITHOUT the time term, so the
       nudge silently vanished for exactly the recipes that mattered. The
       0.05 had never worked as described.
   (2) BUG IN MY SWEEP SCRIPT: I patched the constant on a name that
       resolved to the FUNCTION (the package re-exports the function
       under the module's name), not the module, so the weight never
       changed. My first sweep showed "no weight changes anything", which
       was my harness, not the system. I added an assertion that the
       patched value is the one in effect.
   Result after fixing both: top-ranked recipe within preference 0/14 at
   0.0, 1/14 at 0.05, 13/14 at 0.15, 13/14 at 0.25. Chosen: 0.15
   (smallest that meets the rule; 0.25 adds nothing; equals the cuisine
   and protein weights).
   A discovery that mattered: the weight controls RANKING, not the final
   recommendation. The reasoning LLM made the final pick and followed the
   ranking only partly (final pick within preference ~50-57%). I tried
   adding soft cook-time wording to the reasoning prompt (15/28 over two
   reps, no gain) and REVERTED it, because a change with no measured
   benefit should not ship. Making the default govern the final pick
   would mean choosing in code and using the model only to explain: an
   architecture decision I flagged, not made.


STAGE 3: THE EXTRACTION-INSTABILITY SAFETY INVESTIGATION
--------------------------------------------------------
Observation: for "vegan, gluten-free, and nut-free", one run extracted
all three restrictions and another extracted only two, at temperature 0.
A silently dropped dietary restriction is a safety failure (a nut allergy
sufferer served a nut dish). D2 only caught it because my hand-authored
constraints were unioned in, meaning the eval would have passed while the
system was unsafe.

I QUANTIFIED BEFORE FIXING: every dietary phrasing in the D2 cases, 10
repetitions each at temperature 0. Nut-free appeared in
dietary_restrictions 0/10 (nut allergy phrasing), 0/10 (vegan, no nuts),
3/10 (vegan, gluten-free, nut-free) and 10/10 (same sentence plus "ready
in 10 minutes"). Every other restriction was 10/10 in every case.
   Cause: NOT omission. The model filed "nut-free" under
   avoid_ingredients as ["nut"] instead of dietary_restrictions. My own
   extraction prompt told it that "a named allergen or category of food"
   belongs in avoid_ingredients, so both fields were legitimate. It
   correlated with phrasing ("allergy" shifted it 100% of the time), not
   with the number or position of restrictions (the two three-restriction
   sentences differed only by a trailing time clause and scored 3/10 vs
   10/10).

AND THEN THE WORSE DISCOVERY UNDERNEATH IT: nothing in the single-
recommendation pipeline ENFORCED avoid_ingredients. They reached the
reasoning prompt as text. The multi-day planner matched exact normalized
names only, so "nut" never connected to "almonds". So a nut allergy that
the model put in the "wrong" field excluded nothing in code. D2 had
passed because the model happened to pick clean recipes.

TWO-LAYER FIX that does not depend on the model choosing a field:
   (1) mealsight/matching/avoid.py: code-level exclusion of recipes
       containing an avoided ingredient or named as an avoided dish.
       Whole-word matching (nut != coconut, egg != eggplant) with
       allergen groups (nut, shellfish, seafood, dairy) and the
       cilantro/coriander pair. Wired into match_rank and the planner.
   (2) mealsight/perception/restrictions.py: a deterministic detector
       that reads the user's OWN WORDS with fixed patterns and unions
       the result with the model's, so a stated restriction cannot be
       lost to the model's field choice. It stands down on
       self-correction cues ("oh wait", "actually") so it cannot re-add
       a restriction the speaker just retracted.
   After the fix nut-free was present 10/10 in every case, and
   extraction accuracy did not regress (69/70 text, 137/140 voice,
   0 polarity inversions).
   The guarantee, stated plainly: a restriction in the covered
   vocabulary stated in the covered phrasings can no longer be lost to
   the model's field choice. A paraphrase outside the patterns still
   depends on the model (and is additionally caught by the avoid
   filter). I wrote the limits down rather than overclaiming.


STAGE 4: CUISINE CASES AND THE "INDIAN VS INDIA" LABEL BUG
-----------------------------------------------------------
The soft cuisine score rested on TWO cases (one hit of two = 0.5), which
is not a measurement. I added 10 cuisine cases (6 attainable with this
pantry, 4 not) labelled BEFORE running, so both the hit path and the
honest-miss path were exercised. Result: headline 0.857 (6 of 7). But
one "miss" and one "hit" were both wrong in an informative way:
   d2_31 ("Indian food tonight"): the system recommended a tomato soup
   and told the user "None of the candidates is Indian cuisine, so that
   request could not be met" while Chicken Handi
   (cuisine "India") was cookable. A confident claim contradicted by the
   system's own data.
   d2_33 ("French food"): a "hit", but by luck. The corpus labels are
   "France" and "India" (country names) mixed with "Italian", "Jamaican"
   (demonyms). Search matched cuisine by exact string, so "French" and
   "Indian" matched NOTHING, cuisine was dropped, and the pick happened to
   be a France recipe. The true attainable rate was 5/7 = 0.714, not
   0.857.
   Fix: ONE shared place (mealsight/matching/cuisine.py) with a family
   for each of the 24 corpus labels (label, country, demonym, variants),
   matched case-insensitively in both directions, used by search, the
   soft scorer and every reasoning fact. Built from the real label list
   (the corpus's distinct cuisine values with counts), with a real-corpus
   test that fails if a seeded label has no family. Three more cases
   (American, Dutch, "from Ireland") for the name forms still untested.
   Result: soft cuisine 1.0 over 9 attainable cases.
   The false-claim guard: the "none of the candidates is X" sentence now
   fires only when no shortlisted candidate matches X by family.


STAGE 5: THE TWELVE DEAD FILTERS (A SAFETY-RELEVANT DESIGN BUG)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Audit prompted by the cuisine bug: where else does exact-string matching
silently match nothing? I compared the extraction vocabulary (17
restrictions) to the tags the corpus actually carries (5: vegetarian,
vegan, dairy_free, gluten_free, nut_free). TWELVE restrictions had zero
corpus tags (pescatarian, peanut-free, shellfish-free, soy-free, egg-
free, low-sodium, low-carb, low-fat, keto, paleo, halal, kosher), and
search applied EVERY restriction as a hard tag filter. So a request
carrying any of those got zero recipes and a REFUSAL, not protection. A
voice memo in my own extraction corpus says "nothing with peanuts, my
daughter's allergic" and the pipeline refused it.
   Fix: mealsight/matching/diet.py is the ONE place that classifies every
   restriction into exactly one of four groups: tag-backed (5, still tag
   filters), ingredient-enforced (peanut-free, shellfish-free, soy-free,
   egg-free, pescatarian, halal, kosher, checked against ingredient lists
   with the avoid matcher and the protein lexicon), or unenforced
   (low-sodium, low-fat, low-carb, keto, paleo, disclosed to the user as
   "NOT ENFORCED", never silently ignored). An import-time assertion
   fails the application if the extraction vocabulary and these groups
   ever differ, so a restriction added later can never quietly become a
   dead filter again.
   DECISIONS I made and can defend:
   * halal and kosher are PARTIAL: pork, alcohol (halal) and pork,
     shellfish, meat-mixed-with-dairy (kosher) are excluded; the answer
     says slaughter/certification cannot be verified from a recipe. I did
     not claim certification.
   * The nutritional restrictions were DECLINED, not filtered. I measured
     first: calculate_nutrition has over 80% ingredient coverage for 189
     of 250 recipes, but the figures come from rough unit-to-gram
     conversions and are wrong even there: a baked-potato dish (Kumpir)
     estimated at 13 g carbs per serving and tagged low_carb; a tomato
     soup at 241 g; sodium from 6 to 33,192 mg per serving; median fat
     60 g per serving. A filter on that returns confidently wrong
     recipes. I had implemented a post-ranking filter FIRST, looked at
     the data, and REMOVED it. The honest behaviour is a recommendation
     plus an explicit "NOT ENFORCED: low-carb" statement.
   * keto: its D2 case was a correct refusal (d2_06) under the dead
     filter; under the new design it is a recommendation that MUST
     disclose non-enforcement. The scorer fails the case if the
     disclosure is missing. Recorded as a correction caused by a design
     change, not chosen to improve a result.
   Result: 12 new cases, all labelled against the cookable list before
   running, including cases where a cookable violating recipe exists so
   the exclusion is genuinely exercised (soy, egg, pescatarian: 13
   cookable violators), not passing by absence. D2: 55/55.

Side tasks in this stage: made GET /api/recipes/search use the same groups
(it still had the dead-filter bug, and its list query parameter was a
latent FastAPI bug: an optional list without Query() is read as a request
BODY on a GET). Audited formatting churn from over-broad `ruff format`
runs: 48 files were AST-identical to HEAD (formatting only), 10 mixed
real changes with formatting, and I PROVED the formatter changed no
semantics by showing ruff's reformat of each HEAD file left its syntax
tree unchanged. Confirmed prompt hashes unchanged.


STAGE 6: DIMENSION 3 (MATCH ACCURACY)
-------------------------------------
Built 30 cases (later 32) from real recipes with small ingredient lists,
expected values derived by hand, as in 3.1. Result of the first run:
27/30, three disagreements, and the interesting thing is who was right.

* d3_13 and d3_19: MY ERROR. I wrote the matched name as "chicken stock"
  and the matcher produced "chicken stock cube". I had assumed "cube" is
  a stripped container word; it is not in the list. The score was right,
  the label was mine. I corrected the expectations with notes and kept
  the first-run result on record.

* d3_28: A REAL MATCHER DEFECT. "Garlic Clove" with garlic in the pantry
  was reported MISSING, because "clove" is not a container, prep or
  cut/variety word and there was no synonym. 25 of 250 recipes (10%) list
  "Garlic Clove". Fix: a synonym, NOT stripping "clove" (which would
  break the spice "cloves"). I then audited all 495 distinct normalized
  ingredient names for the same shape (a quantity or form word fused into
  the name). Made synonyms: garlic clove, basil leaf, tarragon leaf,
  fennel bulb, mozzarella ball, chicken/vegetable stock cube. Deliberately
  NOT collapsed (genuinely different): bay leaf, cinnamon stick, lemon
  juice/zest, orange zest, gelatine leaf. Pinned both lists with tests.

* The "untested gap" I had spotted: recipe "chicken stock cube" vs
  pantry "chicken stock". I added two cases (both directions) BEFORE
  fixing. Result: both wrong; one meant a pantry holding stock cubes could
  not cook a recipe whose critical ingredient is chicken stock
  (can_cook=false). Fixed via the synonyms above.

* The mutation-test result (section 3.5): removing PROTECTED_TERMS changed
  nothing. Analysis: the other layers (the cut/variety rule and the
  synonym table) already guard "sweet potato vs potato" and "cream vs
  cream cheese". The only protected term whose protection can change an
  outcome is "hot sauce" (because "hot" is stripped as a prep word). I
  replaced the weakest case with a real-recipe case (Kapsalon) that FAILS
  when protection is removed, and documented the swap in the case file. I
  also stated that the cream-cheese case stays insensitive even then,
  because "cream" resolves to heavy cream by synonym.

* THE FOURTH "CORRECT CODE NEVER CALLED CORRECTLY" INSTANCE. While
  deriving the substitution-rejection case I noticed match_rank called
  the matcher without the user's dietary restrictions, so the matcher's
  dietary rejection of substitutes never ran in production. A dairy-free
  user with Greek yogurt in the pantry was offered it as a mayonnaise
  substitute. I AUDITED EVERY matcher call site (match_rank, the search
  viability probe, the meal planner, the MCP tool, the cook endpoint, the
  public API, search pre-ranking) rather than patching one. Passing the
  restrictions blindly would have made it WORSE: the matcher compared
  restrictions against tags, so "peanut-free" (no tag) would have rejected
  every substitute. I extended the matcher so tag-backed restrictions use
  tags, ingredient-enforced ones use the ingredient rules, and
  unverifiable ones never veto a substitute; avoid lists also block
  substitutes. Verified with a real request (before: Greek yogurt offered;
  after: not offered; control with no restriction: still offered).
  D3: 32/32.


STAGE 7: DIMENSIONS 4 AND 5, AND THE ISOLATION LAYER
-----------------------------------------------------
Needed: exact pantry and profile states per case without touching real
data (section 3.4). I added an env option to the MCP client manager,
built the IsolatedWorld harness, and smoke-tested it end to end (reset
counts all zero, seeded state read back, pipeline sees the isolated
state, real DB fingerprints unchanged) before building cases on it.

D4 DESIGN. 15 cases covering a single expiring item, many users of an
item, two expiring items, an item no recipe uses, a long-expired item
(waste, not an opportunity), the grace boundary (-2 usable, -3 waste),
the threshold edge (3 days counts, 4 does not), an expiring item whose
only recipe is uncookable, a specificity case (expiring "chicken thigh"
vs recipe "chicken"), and two "justified deprioritization" cases built by
searching for an expiring item used only by a marginal recipe (cabbage,
lemon). Verdicts: no_opportunity / used / justified_deprioritization /
failed_to_prioritize.

D4 RESULTS AND WHAT IT TAUGHT:
   * First run 12/15. One was MY bug: asking for butter to expire left it
     fresh because butter is also a staple in my helper. The case-defect
     check caught it ("designed as must_prioritize but the pantry produces
     no_opportunity"). Fixed the helper, added a regression test.
   * Real failures (d4_06, d4_09, later d4_13): I diagnosed by printing
     the system's OWN ranking. match_rank ranked the expiring-item recipe
     FIRST with a composite equal to my hand value to the digit (0.705,
     0.552, 0.775). Then the reasoning LLM overrode it, choosing Kumpir
     "for its cook time" or Pancakes as "the best available match". So:
     the deterministic ranking was right; the LLM stage overrode it. This
     is a recurring theme.
   * One real MECHANISM bug that did not change an outcome: the
     freshness flag compared the RAW pantry name to the RECIPE's canonical
     name, so an expiring "chicken thigh" used by a recipe line "chicken"
     earned no bonus. Flag accuracy 54/55 until fixed.

D5 DESIGN. Pairs of recipes that TIE EXACTLY under default scoring
(both 1.0 matches, same cook-time class, neutral cuisine), so only the
profile can break the tie, and the SAME pair run with OPPOSITE profiles
(Italian loved vs Japanese loved) so a system that ignores the profile
cannot pass both by luck. For dislikes, the disliked ingredient sits in
the recipe default scoring would pick. Cuisine preferences are seeded
through the real learning path (log meals with ratings), 10 ratings per
cuisine (a confident preference; scores 0.885 and 0.115 under the
shrink-toward-neutral prior of 3), and a 1-rating control.

D5 RESULTS: first run 9/15, corrected harness 11/15, FAIL against 0.80.
   * d5_01, d5_03 (cuisine): learned cuisine preference had NO effect.
     Root cause confirmed directly: _cuisine_score looked up the
     lower-cased cuisine, but profile keys keep the capitalization the
     meal was logged with ("Italian"). I demonstrated it in one line:
     {'Italian': 0.885} scores 0.5; {'italian': 0.885} scores 0.885. The
     product logs the corpus label with its capital, so cuisine learning
     had NEVER worked outside tests.
   * d5_07 (protein): the profile's protein_preference was neither merged
     into the request nor shown to the model, so the protein ranking term
     (added in stage 1) worked for a stated protein and was unreachable
     from the profile.
   * d5_06 (dislike): stored dislikes were enforced only by the model
     reading one prompt line. d5_04 and d5_05 passed, d5_06 did not.
     That is precisely "obeys it sometimes".
   * Two passes were LUCK (d5_02, d5_08): the system's own ranking tied
     and they happened to be ordered right. Their opposite-profile
     partners failed, which is exactly what the paired design is for. I
     added a metric (passed_without_scoring_support) so lucky passes are
     visible.
   * The finding I rate most important: a PROBE (unscored) with a saved
     profile restriction "vegetarian" recommended a salmon dish. Fusion
     read only servings and cook time from the profile. A saved nut
     allergy would have been dropped identically.

D5 FIXES (after auditing EVERY profile field, not patching one):
   * Saved dietary_restrictions: unioned with the request in fusion so a
     saved restriction takes the identical path as a stated one (tag
     filters, ingredient rules, substitutes, explanation), in the
     recommendation pipeline AND the multi-day planner. An unrecognized
     saved phrase is kept and disclosed, never dropped.
   * Saved dislikes: enforced in code in match_rank. DECISION: a dislike
     is weaker than an allergy. A recipe containing one is left out only
     while at least one other cookable option remains; if every cookable
     option contains it, they are kept, flagged and disclosed rather than
     recommending nothing. Why not a ranking penalty alone: the evaluation
     had just shown the LLM picks from the list without reliably
     following the ranking, so a penalty would not hold. Why not a hard
     exclusion: it could leave the user with nothing.
   * Cuisine: fixed at the BOUNDARY, not with one .lower(): scores are
     keyed by canonical cuisine label where they are WRITTEN (scoring) and
     READ (profile), and every consumer looks up by cuisine FAMILY.
     Migration: none needed. Legacy rows are re-keyed on read and merged
     as a data-point-weighted mean, and the scoring step rewrites the
     whole cuisine dimension from meal history on every rating; the real
     database held one row already in canonical form.
   * Protein preference: merged as a default a stated protein overrides,
     through the same path as the restrictions.
   * Freshness flag: compare canonical forms and honour specificity.
   * Added d5_16 (saved vegetarian), d5_17 (saved peanut-free,
     ingredient-enforced), d5_18 (a dislike with no alternative must
     still be recommended, not refused). The original 15 are untouched
     and the addition is recorded in the case file.
   RESULTS: D5 18/18 with the system's own ranking matching my hand
   ranking 14/14 (no lucky passes). D4 14/15 on three consecutive runs
   (previously it varied 12-14 because of model nondeterminism).

A FALSE ALARM IN MY OWN INTEGRITY CHECK (good story). One D5 scorecard
said real_data_untouched=false and FAIL despite 18/18. I did not wave it
away. The real pantry's row-level content hash was identical to the
seeded fixture's; only the FILE BYTES had changed, because I had run the
full test suite while the evaluation was in progress and opening a SQLite
database in WAL mode checkpoints the file without changing a row. My byte
hash was too fragile. I changed the check to a hash of every table's
rows and added a test that proves it ignores a checkpoint but catches a
real row change. I also corrected a diagnostic metric twice (it wrongly
flagged exclusion-type cases because I compared against a ranking that
included the excluded recipes).

6. MY OWN MISTAKES (TELL THESE HONESTLY, THEY BUILD TRUST)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
An interviewer who hears only successes is suspicious. These are real,
and each one was caught by a mechanism I had built, which is the point.

1. Wrong expectations authored in D2 (d2_10, d2_12, d2_30). Caught by
   examining the cookable list. Lesson: verify an authored "this should
   be refused" against the data, and a case that passes for the wrong
   reason is the most dangerous kind.
2. A case-construction bug (butter is a staple, so "expiring butter" was
   fresh). Caught by the case-defect check. Lesson: the harness must
   validate its own cases.
3. The nudge weight: I picked 0.05 unmeasured; it was also silently
   disabled for the top candidates by my own re-score code; and my first
   sweep patched the wrong object so it reported "no effect". Lesson:
   assert that the thing you changed is the thing in effect; pre-register
   the selection rule.
4. D3: I mis-derived two expected labels ("cube" is not a container
   word). Lesson: hand derivation can be wrong too; keep the first-run
   result on record and annotate corrections.
5. D3 test quality: the first protected-term cases could not fail. Found
   only because I mutation-tested my own tests.
6. A byte-level database fingerprint produced a false alarm. Lesson:
   choose the invariant that actually means "untouched" (rows, not bytes).
7. A diagnostic metric flagged the wrong cases (twice).
8. Tooling slips: a helper function called with the wrong number of
   arguments silently skipped an edit and I started a run without the
   notes I intended; a stray `cat` waiting on stdin hung a command; an
   over-broad `ruff format` produced 48 formatting-only file changes
   which I then audited and proved semantically empty; an over-broad
   process-kill pattern was (rightly) blocked, so I killed by PID.
   Lesson: scope shell commands tightly; verify an edit actually applied.
9. A new test hung because it did not close its database connection (its
   neighbours did).


---------------------------------------------------------------------
7. THE PRINCIPLES (WHAT I CAN GENERALIZE)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. MEASURE BEFORE YOU FIX. Diagnose and report first; change after. I
   quantified the extraction drop rate (10 reps x every phrasing) before
   touching the prompt, swept the nudge weight before choosing it, and
   measured nutrition across all 250 recipes before deciding not to
   filter on it.
2. AN ORACLE MUST BE INDEPENDENT OF WHAT IT TESTS. Hand-derived values;
   a separate literal copy of the ranking weights; the scorer's own copy
   of the restriction term lists (so it checks production, not mirrors
   it).
3. SEPARATE "SYSTEM WRONG" FROM "TEST WRONG". Case-defect checks;
   first-run results kept alongside corrections.
4. JUDGE AGAINST WHAT WAS POSSIBLE. Compute the cookable set first.
5. TEST YOUR TESTS. Mutation testing; known-answer scorer fixtures;
   a test that the integrity check itself ignores noise and catches real
   change.
6. FIND THE SEAM, NOT THE FUNCTION. Four times the right code was never
   called correctly. When it happened, I AUDITED every call site and
   every profile field instead of patching one.
7. PRE-REGISTER AND NEVER MOVE A THRESHOLD. A suite that adjusts its bar
   to pass is not a suite.
8. DISCLOSE WHAT YOU CANNOT ENFORCE. Nutritional restrictions are
   declined with an explicit statement. The system must never imply a
   restriction is satisfied when it cannot verify it.
9. FIX AT THE BOUNDARY. Cuisine keys canonicalized where they are stored
   and read, via shared families, not a .lower() at one call site.
10. PROVENANCE OR IT DIDN'T HAPPEN. Every scorecard is traceable; I also
    wrote down where provenance falls short (logic is not hashed).
11. HONEST FRAMING OF NONDETERMINISM. The final pick is an LLM choice;
    I ran D4 three times and reported the spread, distinguished
    outcome-pass from scoring-supported-pass, and did not retry until a
    run looked good.

9. LIKELY INTERVIEW QUESTIONS AND HOW I WOULD ANSWER
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. "Tell me about a technically hard problem you solved."
A. The safety-relevant one: a saved dietary restriction was stored
   correctly and never applied. I found it with an unscored probe after
   my personalization suite flagged that cuisine and protein profile
   signals did nothing. The fix was not one line: I audited every field
   on the profile and every consumer, found four profile fields not
   reaching the pipeline, and fixed them at the seam (fusion unions saved
   restrictions with the request so they take the identical path as
   stated ones). I added scored cases and verified with a real request
   that a saved vegetarian now yields a vegetarian recommendation.

Q2. "How do you test an LLM-based system?"
A. Separate the deterministic layers from the model and test each with
   the right tool. Deterministic code (matching, ranking, filtering) gets
   hand-derived known-answer tests with independent oracles and mutation
   testing. The model's behaviour gets scenario evaluations judged against
   what was possible, with nondeterminism handled by repeats and by
   reporting spread, not a single lucky run. And I record whether a pass
   came from the scoring or from the model's luck.

Q3. "How did you keep your tests from being tautological?"
A. I derived expected values by hand before running anything, recorded a
   hash of the case file prior to the first run, and kept a separate
   literal copy of the ranking weights for the ranking checks. For D3 I
   also wrote a test that applies the documented formula to each case's
   own expected buckets to catch my arithmetic.

Q4. "Describe a time you were wrong."
A. Several, in section 6. The best one: I chose a ranking weight of 0.05
   without measuring, it turned out to be silently disabled for the top
   candidates by my own re-score code, and my first sweep showed 'no
   effect' because it patched the wrong object. I fixed both, asserted
   the patched value was in effect, and chose the weight by a rule I had
   fixed before running.

Q5. "How do you handle nondeterminism?"
A. I measure it. Extraction at temperature 0 still varied: I ran every
   phrasing 10 times to quantify it and found the model moved a
   restriction between fields. I then made safety not depend on that
   choice (code-level enforcement plus a deterministic detector). For
   the final recommendation I ran D4 three times, reported the spread,
   and added a metric that distinguishes outcome passes from
   scoring-supported passes.

Q6. "Why did you not lower the Dimension 1 threshold?"
A. Because the cause is a model-availability problem, not a code
   regression, and a threshold exists to say what 'good enough' means. If
   I move it to fit the model I convert a failing result into a passing
   one without improving anything. The document explains the cause
   (retired model, free-plan limits) and the dimension stays red until a
   stronger vision model is reachable.

Q7. "How did you make sure the evaluation didn't corrupt real data?"
A. Isolated databases for the stateful dimensions, a reset that asserts
   zero rows in every table, read-back verification of seeded state, and
   content fingerprints of the real databases before and after. When that
   check false-alarmed on a WAL checkpoint, I changed it to hash rows
   instead of bytes and tested that it ignores checkpoints but catches a
   real change.

Q8. "What was the most valuable bug the suite found, and why wasn't it
    caught by unit tests?"
A. Saved restrictions ignored (safety), or cuisine learning dead
   (silent). Both had passing unit tests because the units were right;
   the defect was at the seam: fusion read two fields from the profile,
   and the scoring code looked up a lower-cased key against capitalized
   keys. Unit tests exercise a function with inputs the author thought
   of; an end-to-end scenario with a seeded profile exposes the wiring.

Q9. "Safety-critical behaviour: how did you handle it?"
A. Hard constraints are binary with a threshold of 1.0. A restriction
   is enforced in code at several layers (tag filter, ingredient rules,
   substitute rejection, the explanation), and anything I cannot enforce
   is disclosed explicitly as NOT ENFORCED rather than implied to be met.
   I declined to filter on nutrition after measuring that its estimates
   were unreliable, and described halal/kosher as partial.

Q10. "A trade-off you made."
A. Dislike strength. Hard exclusion could leave nothing cookable; a
   ranking penalty would not hold because the LLM does not reliably
   follow the ranking. I chose: exclude while another cookable option
   remains, otherwise keep, flag and disclose. It is testable (d5_18
   checks the fallback) and does not depend on the model noticing a line.

Q11. "How did you decide what counts as a failure when the system can't
    do the thing?"
A. By computing what was possible first. An expiring ingredient no
   cookable recipe uses is not a failure; a better-matching recipe
   correctly beating a marginal one is not a failure. Only failing to
   use an available, better-or-equal option counts.

Q12. "What would you do differently?"
A. Hash the code logic, not just lexicons and prompts, so provenance
   explains behavioural changes. Build a richer pantry fixture (only 22
   of 250 recipes are cookable, so many passes are statements about this
   pantry). Decide how much the final LLM choice may depart from the
   ranking (d4_13). Add scored cases for the meal planner. And I would
   have measured the nudge weight and the extraction behaviour before
   shipping the first version, not after.

Q13. "How do you know your fix didn't break anything?"
A. Full test suite each time (786 -> 936 tests), the dimension re-runs
   compared per case with the previous run (for D3: fixed d3_28, two
   new passes, zero regressions), and extraction accuracy re-measured
   after the extraction changes (69/70 and 137/140 unchanged).

Q14. "Tell me about a design decision at a system boundary."
A. Cuisine keys. I could have added .lower() at the one broken call site.
   Instead I canonicalized the key where scores are written and read,
   used shared cuisine families, and confirmed no data migration was
   needed (legacy rows are re-keyed on read and the scorer rebuilds the
   dimension from history on every rating).

Q15. "How do you communicate bad news?"
A. Plainly and with evidence. The final scorecard says FAIL, the first
   line of the standing document names the failing dimension and its
   cause, and the caveats section lists that D2 was re-baselined
   post hoc, that the pantry is thin, and that the vision benchmark used
   a different prompt format from production.

10. WHAT NOT TO OVERCLAIM
---------------------------------------------------------------------
* D2's move from 20/30 to 55/55 is a post-hoc re-baseline after wrong
  expectations were corrected and cases and pipeline changes were made;
  it is not a blind result. Say so before they ask.
* The pantry fixture makes only 22 of 250 recipes cookable, so passes are
  about this pantry.
* D4 is not fully fixed: the reasoning stage still overrides a correct
  freshness ranking (d4_13), identically every run.
* Some D3 expectations rest on intent, not documented rules (garlic
  clove, stock cube); they are labelled and reported separately.
* Provenance hashes do not cover logic changes.
* The vision benchmark prompt format differs from production's JSON
  variant; D1's headline measures a near neighbour of what ships.
* Small samples: 15-55 cases per dimension; one case moves D4 by 0.067.
* The suite tested one model on the free plan; nothing here speaks to a
  larger model.
* The nutrition "4x" bug: I confirmed the mechanism from the code change
  (per-serving values were not scaled to the requested servings); do not
  claim which dimension found it unless I can show it.
* "About sixteen bugs" is the count of distinct defects in the catalogue
  above (items 1-16, plus 17-18 and the scorer defects); be ready to show
  the list.
