PHASE 2 STUDY NOTES: THE RECIPE ENGINE
=============================================================

These notes cover the second backend phase: the ingredient matching
algorithm, the verification pass that found and fixed real bugs in it
(and in data left over from phase 1.4), and the five Recipe Engine tools
built on top of all of it. Everything below describes code and data that
really exist right now, in backend/mealsight/matching/,
backend/mealsight/recipe_engine/, backend/mealsight/seed/, and
backend/tests/.

This phase happened in three rounds, and the notes are organized the
same way: first the matcher itself, then the verification pass that
found real defects in it (Phase 2.2, a name that shows up directly in
several docstrings and test files in this codebase), then the nutrition-
coverage gap that verification pass surfaced plus the Recipe Engine tools
built afterward.


1. WHAT THIS PHASE BUILT AND WHY IT COMES NOW
=============================================================

Phase 1 built the plumbing (databases, config, logging) and phase 1.4
filled recipes.db with 250 real recipes plus three reference tables
(substitutions, ingredient_synonyms, nutrition_reference). None of that
data does anything on its own - something has to actually compare a
recipe's ingredient list against what's really in someone's pantry and
decide whether the recipe is makeable. That's what this phase builds:
mealsight.matching, the core algorithm, and mealsight.recipe_engine, five
plain-Python tools that use it (and the recipe data directly) to answer
the concrete questions an agent will eventually need answered - which
recipes fit these constraints, what's actually in one of them, how do I
cook it for six people instead of four, what does it add up to
nutritionally, and what do I use instead of the one ingredient I don't
have.

Every single function in both packages is deterministic and rule-based -
no LLM call anywhere, same as phase 1.4's seed pipeline. The reasoning
for that is identical to before: this logic needs to produce the same
answer for the same input every time, needs to be fast and free to run
per-request rather than per-token-billed, and needs to be debuggable by
reading a rule instead of guessing why a model said what it said.

3. THE INGREDIENT MATCHING PROBLEM
=============================================================

Why you can't just compare ingredient strings directly
------------------------------------------------------
A recipe says "Chopped tomatoes." A pantry item says "tomato." A vision
model looking at a photo says "canned tomatoes." All three mean the same
real thing, but as plain strings they're all different, and a naive
string-equality check would call the recipe unmakeable even though the
pantry clearly has what's needed. Solving this well needs three separate
layers, in order: normalize away the noise that isn't part of an
ingredient's identity (packaging, prep verbs, plurals - section 4);
resolve real synonyms, different names for the literal same thing
(section 5's "scallion" versus "green onion"); and handle specificity,
where two names refer to the same ingredient but at different levels of
detail (section 6's "chicken" versus "chicken thighs"). Only after all
three does it make sense to ask "does the pantry have what this recipe
needs."


4. NORMALIZATION
=============================================================

normalize_ingredient (mealsight/matching/normalize.py) lowercases,
strips punctuation, removes container/packaging words (carton, jar,
bottle, bottled, jarred, canned, boxed, bagged, of, a - the "bottled"/
"jarred"/"boxed"/"bagged" adjectival forms were a gap found and fixed in
phase 2.3, section 7), removes preparation modifiers that don't change
identity (chopped, diced, minced, sliced, fresh, frozen, cooked, raw,
plus several more found by inspecting real seeded ingredient text -
freshly, cubed, grated, beaten, chilled, and others), and singularizes
plurals.

The one rule that overrides everything else: PROTECTED_TERMS, a frozen
set of multi-word ingredients (sweet potato, cream cheese, green onion,
coconut milk, soy sauce, hot sauce, and about twenty more) that must
never be decomposed down to one of their own words. A sweet potato is
not a potato; cream cheese is not cream; hot sauce is not just any sauce
served hot. Before container/prep-word stripping ever runs,
_protect_multi_word_terms glues any contiguous run of words matching a
protected phrase into one token, so the later stripping pass can't reach
inside and break it apart.

    PROTECTED_TERMS includes ("hot", "sauce") specifically because
    "hot" was already in the generic preparation-modifier list (for
    "hot water", "hot milk" - genuinely the same thing served hot).
    Without the override, "hot sauce" collapsed all the way down to
    bare "sauce" - a real bug found in phase 2.3 that had been silently
    breaking one particular synonym pair (canonical "hot sauce", synonym
    "hotsauce") since phase 1.4, invisible until coverage checking
    started actually exercising synonym resolution.


5. SYNONYM RESOLUTION
=============================================================

resolve_canonical (mealsight/matching/synonyms.py) is a single dict
lookup: an already-normalized name in, its canonical form out, unchanged
if it isn't a known synonym of anything. load_synonym_map loads every
(canonical_name, synonym) pair from ingredient_synonyms.json once per
process and caches it - the same load-once-per-process shape
mealsight.db.connection uses for its Database singletons. Lookup is
exact-match on the normalized form, never substring, specifically so a
synonym entry for "chicken" can never make "chicken stock" resolve as if
it partially matched "chicken."


6. SPECIFICITY MATCHING
=============================================================

The problem this solves
------------------------------------------------------
Phase 2.2's demo run against a real pantry (eggs, rice, soy sauce,
garlic, onion, spinach, chicken thighs, olive oil, butter, milk) scored
Chicken Alfredo Primavera 0.00 with can_cook=False, reporting "missing
critical: chicken" - even though the pantry clearly had chicken, just as
chicken thighs specifically. Normalization alone can't fix this: "thigh"
isn't a container word or a meaningless prep verb, it's a real fact about
which cut of chicken this is, and stripping it away would be wrong for
other purposes. This needed its own comparison logic, added in phase
2.2's fix: mealsight/matching/specificity.py.

The rule
------------------------------------------------------
compare_specificity takes two already-normalized names and returns
"full", "partial", or "none":

    If the pantry item has every word the recipe requirement has, plus
    extra words that are all recognized CUT_VARIETY_TERMS (a cut of
    meat/fish - thigh, breast, fillet, drumstick, and about a dozen
    more - or a color/size variety - red, yellow, white, baby) - the
    pantry item is MORE specific than the requirement, and that's a full
    match. "chicken thighs" satisfies "chicken"; "salmon fillet"
    satisfies "salmon"; "red onion" satisfies "onion".

    If it's the other way - the recipe requirement has the extra
    cut/variety words the pantry item lacks - the pantry item is LESS
    specific, and that's a partial match: scored at
    settings.substitution_match_weight (0.7), surfaced in a dedicated
    partial_matches list on MatchResult with a note explaining the user
    may not have the right cut.

CUT_VARIETY_TERMS is deliberately kept disjoint from PROTECTED_TERMS'
vocabulary (no word used to build a protected phrase - sweet, cream,
coconut, green, spring - ever appears in CUT_VARIETY_TERMS), and
compare_specificity has a defensive check on top of that: it refuses to
call two names a specificity match if the fuller one's exact word
combination is itself a protected term, even if one of its words happens
to coincide with a cut/variety word. In today's data that check never
actually fires - it's a backstop against a future edit to either list
accidentally creating an overlap, not something that's needed yet.

Priority order in the matcher
------------------------------------------------------
match_recipe checks, per ingredient, in this order: exact canonical
match, then full specificity match, then a table-driven substitution
(section 8's substitutions.json), then a partial specificity match, then
finally missing. A table-driven substitution outranks a partial
specificity match on the reasoning that an authored substitution comes
with a real ratio and flavor-impact note, which is a better
recommendation than just "you have a less specific version of this."


7. THE PHASE 2.2 VERIFICATION PASS AND THE BUGS IT FOUND
=============================================================

Phase 2.2 was a deliberate re-check of what phase 2.1 and phase 1.4 had
built, not new feature work - and it found three real, previously-
shipped bugs, all from the exact same root cause: term-matching that
checked for a raw substring instead of a whole word.

"egg" inside "reggiano"
------------------------------------------------------
assign_importances (mealsight/seed/recipe_parsing.py) picks one
ingredient per recipe as "critical" - the first one matching the
recipe's own title, or failing that, the first one matching a
PROTEIN_TERMS word. The check used to be plain `term in name` - and
"reggiano" (from "Parmigiano-Reggiano") contains the substring "egg" at
positions 1-4, purely by coincidence of spelling, with no real egg
anywhere in the dish. Spicy Arrabiata Penne, a tomato-and-chili pasta,
ended up with parmesan cheese as its one "critical" ingredient instead
of the pasta or the sauce. Fixed with _matches_any_term_whole_word,
using \b-bounded regex matching instead of raw containment - "egg" no
longer matches inside "reggiano" since there's no word boundary between
the "r" before it and the "i" after it.

"salt" inside "Salt Cod"
------------------------------------------------------
A different failure mode, needing a different fix: "Salt Cod" (a real,
critical protein ingredient - salt-cured dried fish) contains the
genuine whole word "salt", which is also a GARNISH_AND_SEASONING_TERMS
entry. Whole-word matching alone doesn't fix this, because "salt"
really is a standalone word here, not a substring collision. The actual
fix: a protein-term match now always outranks a garnish-term match, so
an ingredient matching both (like "Salt Cod", matching "cod" as a
protein and "salt" as a seasoning) is treated as a real ingredient, not
downgraded to optional. This also incidentally fixed "Unsalted Butter",
which had the same "salt" collision.

"meat" missing from MEAT_TERMS
------------------------------------------------------
The very first thing scripts/audit_recipe_data.py's dietary-tag-safety
check found (before any of the above): Kapsalon, a recipe containing
"Doner Meat", was tagged vegetarian. MEAT_TERMS listed specific meat
names (chicken, beef, goat, mince, and so on) but never the generic word
"meat" itself, so "Doner Meat" matched nothing on the blocklist. Fixed
by adding "meat" to MEAT_TERMS - verified against the full corpus first
to confirm no other ingredient (goat meat, mincemeat) would newly
collide, since both of those already matched via "goat" and "mince"
respectively.

Importance-heuristic investigation
------------------------------------------------------
Separately, phase 2.2 investigated a specific complaint: Spanish
Tortilla scored 0.60/can_cook=True while missing potato, when potato is
what defines a Spanish tortilla (a potato omelette). Reviewing ten
randomly sampled recipes (Python's random.seed(42) against the real
corpus) found two clear substring-matching bugs (both fixed above) and a
separate, genuine limitation: assign_importances can only ever mark
something "critical" via a literal title-text match or a PROTEIN_TERMS
match - there's no equivalent list for non-protein staples (potato,
rice, pasta shape names) that can define a dish just as much as a
protein can. Potato in Spanish Tortilla, and a similar gap in two other
sampled recipes (Fettucine alfredo, Baingan Bharta, where no ingredient
became critical at all), are the same root limitation. This was
deliberately left unfixed - see section 11 - since a real fix needs a
genuinely subjective judgment call about which staples count, and the
task's own instruction was not to over-engineer a fix without a clear,
mechanical pattern the way the substring bugs had one.


8. THE SUBSTITUTIONS DATASET AND MATCH SCORING
=============================================================

(The substitutions.json data itself - 57 entries, dietary_notes safety
testing, the ghee/honey traps - was built in phase 1.4 and is unchanged
here; see that phase's notes for the full data-quality story. This
section covers how match_recipe actually uses it.)

For each recipe ingredient not directly matched or specificity-matched,
match_recipe looks up substitution_map[canonical] and filters to options
where the substitute is (a) something the pantry actually has, resolved
through the same normalize-then-synonym-resolve pipeline, and (b) safe
under every requested dietary restriction, checked via
substitute_satisfies_dietary_restrictions - which reuses
mealsight.seed.recipe_parsing.derive_dietary_tags directly on the
substitute's own name, rather than trusting a separate authored field.
The lowest flavor_impact-ranked eligible option wins (minimal beats
noticeable beats significant).

Scoring:

    base = (matched_count + substitutable_count*0.7 + partial_count*0.7)
           / total_required_ingredients
    penalty = 0.3 * count_of_missing_critical_ingredients
    score = clamp(base - penalty, 0.0, 1.0)
    can_cook = score >= 0.5 AND no critical ingredient missing

(0.7 and 0.3 and 0.5 are settings.substitution_match_weight,
settings.critical_missing_penalty, and settings.min_ingredient_match
respectively - real, configured thresholds, not hardcoded literals.)
can_cook requires both conditions, not just the score threshold, because
a recipe can mathematically clear 0.5 while still being missing
something the whole dish doesn't work without - nine matched ingredients
out of ten, with the tenth being a missing critical one, scores 0.6 by
the formula above but must still not be cookable.


9. THE NUTRITION COVERAGE SAGA
=============================================================

First pass: 25.6% to 100% (recipe corpus only)
------------------------------------------------------
Phase 2.2's audit found nutrition_reference covering only 25.6% of
distinct recipe ingredients (74.4% weighted by how often each one
actually appears). Most of the gap was genuinely missing data - 373 new
entries were added (nutrition_data.json, 136 to 509 entries at that
point), reaching 100% distinct and 100% occurrence-weighted coverage
against the 250-recipe corpus. A real internal-consistency bug was found
along the way: nutrition_data.json itself had 8 pairs of near-duplicate
keys (e.g. "onion" and "onions", "flour" and "plain flour") that
silently collapsed into one row when loaded (a plain Python dict
comprehension keeps only the last key it sees), quietly discarding half
the authored data for each pair.

Second pass: the population itself was wrong
------------------------------------------------------
Phase 2.3 started from one specific finding: "white rice" had no
nutrition_reference row, despite the recipe-corpus coverage figure
already reading 100%. The reason: that 100% figure only ever measured
ingredient strings appearing in TheMealDB's own recipe text. The vision
model (built in an earlier phase, benchmarked in
scripts/benchmark_vision.py) produces its own vocabulary - "white rice",
"chicken thigh", "scallions" - that never appears in TheMealDB at all,
so a coverage check scoped only to the recipe corpus can read 100% while
still being unusable for anything photo-derived.

mealsight/seed/vision_vocabulary.py was built to close this measurement
gap: extract_vision_ingredient_names parses the real, checkpointed
output of every benchmark run (backend/.cache/vision_benchmark_
checkpoint.jsonl, gitignored, produced by actually running
scripts/benchmark_vision.py) into a frequency-weighted Counter of raw
ingredient-name guesses, handling every prompt-variant's output shape
(plain numbered lists, "item | confidence: high | location: ..."
annotations, bracketed "<item> | ..." forms) and filtering genuine
preamble sentences ("Here is the list of food items I can clearly see
in the image:") without discarding real bare-line items that happen to
lack a numbering prefix.

Extracting and checking that vocabulary surfaced a second, more
structural bug: the coverage check itself never applied synonym
resolution. It compared raw normalized recipe-ingredient strings
directly against raw nutrition_reference keys - which happened to work
for the recipe corpus (mostly) but silently broke the moment a synonym
was actually involved. Concretely: a nutrition row for "green onion"
had been filed under "scallion" instead (its own literal key, not the
table's actual canonical target) during the first coverage pass, and
this was invisible until resolution was actually exercised. Once
load_nutrition.py's coverage_report was rewritten to resolve through
ingredient_synonyms the same way a real nutrition lookup would (normalize,
then resolve_canonical, then check nutrition_reference), nine more of
these same mismatches surfaced at once - all pre-existing, all from
phase 1.4's original 83-entry synonym file, where the canonical names
chosen (American spellings: eggplant, shrimp, arugula, chili, baking
soda, powdered sugar, self rising flour, cider vinegar) never actually
matched the nutrition rows filed under their British-spelled equivalents
(aubergine, prawn, rocket, chilli, bicarbonate soda, icing sugar,
self-raising flour, apple cider vinegar). Every one of these was a
rename - one real ingredient, one row, filed under whichever name its
own synonym table actually calls canonical - rather than a new,
duplicate entry.

Real numbers, before and after each correction
------------------------------------------------------
Naive lowercase-only normalizer (the original measurement): 25.6%
distinct / 74.4% weighted.

Matcher's real normalizer applied consistently, no synonym resolution
yet: 25.7% distinct / 75.9% weighted - barely moved, confirming most of
the original gap was real missing data, not measurement error.

531 nutrition entries added, still no synonym resolution in the
check: 100.0% / 100.0% (recipe corpus).

Synonym resolution turned on in the coverage check: dropped to 98.1% /
98.1% - not a regression in the data, a correction in the measurement,
immediately surfacing the nine mismatched-canonical rows above.

After renaming all nine (plus the "hot sauce" case from section 4) to
their real canonical names: 483/483 (100.0%) distinct, 2648/2648
(100.0%) weighted - recipe corpus, final.

Vision vocabulary, measured for the first time ever in this phase:
51/150 (34.0%) distinct, 1583/1849 (85.6%) weighted. The gap between
those two numbers is the story: a large fraction of distinct vision
outputs are one-off hedging noise from weaker prompt variants under
test ("red with content unclear", "what appear to be red sauce or
puree", brand-name label misreads like "Chavroux cheese") that no
synonym table or nutrition row can sensibly resolve, but the bulk of
actual occurrences - the ingredients the model actually, confidently
named - are well covered.

64 new ingredient_synonyms.json entries were added (83 to 147) mapping
real vision-vocabulary phrasing to existing canonicals (white rice,
long grain rice -> rice; baby carrot -> carrot; half and half cream/
milk/or cream -> heavy cream; and so on), and a further ~28 nutrition
rows were added or renamed, reaching the numbers above. Deliberately not
touched: brown rice and jasmine rice, which already had their own
accurate dedicated rows before this phase and would have lost precision
if redirected to generic "rice" just for consistency with the white-rice
fix.

The coverage-regression guard
------------------------------------------------------
backend/tests/test_seed/test_nutrition_coverage.py now checks both
populations separately: recipe-corpus occurrence-weighted coverage must
stay at or above 90%, vision-vocabulary occurrence-weighted coverage at
or above 80% (deliberately lower than the recipe-corpus bar, since a
meaningful chunk of real vision output is inherent, unresolvable
hedging noise, documented inline in the test). Both checks read the
real, already-seeded database (and, for the vision check, the real
checkpoint file on disk) rather than a synthetic fixture; both skip
cleanly rather than failing on a machine where the prerequisite hasn't
been generated yet.


10. THE RECIPE ENGINE TOOLS
=============================================================

search_recipes
------------------------------------------------------
Queries recipes by cook time, cuisine, and meal_type in SQL directly,
then filters by dietary_filters in Python (dietary_tags is stored as a
JSON column, not queryable by SQL directly). This filter is a hard
exclusion, not a ranking signal: a recipe missing even one requested tag
is dropped from the result set entirely, never included but sorted
lower - there's no such thing as "almost vegan" in this function.
Recipes with a null cook_time_minutes are excluded from a max_cook_time
filter outright, since there's no way to confirm they actually satisfy
it. Returns compact RecipeSummary objects - no ingredient matching here,
that's what mealsight.matching is for.

get_recipe
------------------------------------------------------
Fetches one recipe in full and parses its JSON columns (ingredients,
steps, dietary_tags) into a typed RecipeDetail. Raises ValueError,
naming the id, if it doesn't exist - the same convention
match_recipe_by_id already established in mealsight.matching.

scale_recipe
------------------------------------------------------
Scales every ingredient's quantity from a recipe's servings_base to a
target serving count, following three rules directly from the task:

    Quantities are always shown as human-readable fractions ("1/4 cup"),
    never raw decimals ("0.25 cups") - _format_quantity uses Python's
    Fraction.limit_denominator(8), snapping to the nearest common cooking
    fraction (eighths or coarser).

    Countable units (clove, can, slice, stick, piece, packet, bunch, or
    no unit at all - a bare "1 onion") round to a whole number after
    scaling, with a minimum of 1 - real cloves of garlic don't come in
    fractions, and scaling down should never make a real ingredient
    disappear. 1.5 cloves scaled by 0.25 (4 servings down to 1) is
    0.375, which rounds to 1, not 0.

    To-taste and dash-style quantities (dash, pinch, handful, or no
    quantity at all) are left completely unscaled - there's nothing
    numeric there to multiply in the first place.

Cook time is only adjusted when the scale factor exceeds 2x or falls
below 0.5x, using a deliberately mild, sublinear formula
(scale_factor**0.3) rather than pretending to compute a precise new
time - doubling a recipe doesn't double how long it takes to cook, but a
much bigger or smaller batch does take somewhat more or less time, and
this heuristic says so explicitly in a returned note rather than
silently changing a number.

calculate_nutrition
------------------------------------------------------
Resolves every ingredient through the exact same normalize-then-
synonym-resolve pipeline the matcher uses, looks up its
nutrition_reference row, converts its quantity to grams via an explicit
UNIT_TO_GRAMS table, and sums calories/protein/carbs/fat/fiber/sodium,
divided by the requested serving count.

The unit-to-grams conversion is a deliberately simple, generic
approximation - volume units (cup, tbsp, ml) are treated at roughly
water density, and countable units (clove, can, piece) use one rough
average weight each - since there's no per-ingredient density table in
this project. This is a real, documented limitation (a cup of flour and
a cup of honey do not weigh the same in reality), not something this
function tries to hide.

Coverage is reported on every single result: ingredients_covered,
ingredients_total, coverage_pct, and a plain-English coverage_note that
says outright when a total was computed from partial data ("Nutrition
calculated from 7 of 8 ingredients (88% coverage) - totals likely
understate the real values"). The three dietary tags (high_protein,
low_carb, low_calorie) are only ever applied when coverage_pct exceeds
80% - a real test in this phase constructs a genuinely high-protein
recipe, drags its coverage down to 25% with three deliberately uncovered
filler ingredients, and confirms the tag is suppressed even though the
underlying protein value would clear the threshold on its own. A tag
built from a hole in the data is worse than no tag, since it looks
exactly as confident as one built from complete data.

find_substitutions
------------------------------------------------------
Looks up every substitutions.json row for one ingredient, sorted by
flavor_impact (minimal first), same as the matcher's own substitute
ranking. reason (unavailable, allergic, dietary, dislike) is recorded on
the result for context, but the actual hard filtering is driven by a
separate dietary_restrictions argument, reusing
substitute_satisfies_dietary_restrictions - the exact same function and
the exact same tag vocabulary (dairy_free, nut_free, and so on) the
matcher itself uses for the identical purpose, rather than a second,
parallel allergy-and-diet system invented just for this one function.


11. HOW THIS CONNECTS TO THE REST, AND WHAT'S DEFERRED
=============================================================

Downstream connections: an agent (a later phase, not built yet) is the
obvious caller of every function in this phase - search_recipes to find
candidates under hard constraints, mealsight.matching.match_recipe_by_id
to score each one against a real pantry, get_recipe/scale_recipe/
calculate_nutrition/find_substitutions to answer follow-up questions
about whichever recipe gets chosen. Nothing in this phase has an MCP
wrapper yet - these are plain, directly-callable Python functions, with
the wrapper explicitly deferred to a later session.

Two findings from this phase were deliberately left unfixed, recorded in
docs/KNOWN_ISSUES.md rather than silently left implicit:

    derive_dietary_tags' EGG_TERMS check still uses raw substring
    matching (the same class of bug assign_importances had, fixed in
    section 7) - "eggplant" would falsely match "egg". Currently masked
    because the seeded corpus says "aubergine", not "eggplant" -
    genuinely live, just not yet triggered by the data on hand.

    Non-protein defining ingredients (potato in Spanish Tortilla) never
    reach "critical" importance, since assign_importances only ever
    checks title-text and PROTEIN_TERMS. Deliberately not fixed without
    a clearer, less subjective pattern to follow - see section 7's
    importance-heuristic investigation.


12. THE TESTS
=============================================================

test_matching/ (61 tests)
------------------------------------------------------
test_normalize.py checks every PROTECTED_TERMS entry survives intact and
never collapses to its own bare component (sweet potato versus potato,
cream cheese versus cream), plus container/prep-word stripping and
plural singularization against real seeded ingredient text.

test_synonyms.py checks exact-match resolution (bell pepper/capsicum
unify; potato and sweet potato do not) and that resolution is exact, not
substring ("chicken" resolving to something must not make "chicken
stock" resolve the same way).

test_specificity.py parametrizes compare_specificity directly across
every case from the task spec (chicken thighs/chicken full, chicken/
chicken thighs partial, sweet potato/potato none both directions, ground
beef/beef full, coconut milk/milk none, red onion/onion full, green
onion/onion none, salmon/salmon fillet full) plus full match_recipe
integration tests confirming the score and partial_matches list come out
right end to end.

test_matcher.py hand-verifies scoring arithmetic on constructed cases -
full match, one critical missing forcing can_cook False even though the
raw score clears 0.5, several optional items missing still allowing
can_cook True, a zero-ingredient recipe scoring 0.0 without a division-
by-zero, dietary-restricted substitution rejection and acceptance, and
that a substitute must actually be available in the pantry, not just
exist in the substitutions table.

test_recipe_engine/ (22 tests)
------------------------------------------------------
test_search.py directly asserts the dietary filter is exclusionary
("a recipe missing the required tag must be excluded outright, not
merely ranked lower" is the literal assertion message), that unknown
cook time excludes a recipe from a max_cook_time filter, and that
get_recipe raises naming the missing id.

test_scaling.py asserts a scaled quantity comes back as "1/2" not "0.5",
that 1.5 cloves scaled to 0.375 becomes "1" with no "/" in it anywhere,
that to-taste and dash quantities pass through completely untouched, and
that cook time is only adjusted outside the 0.5x-2x band (both a case
inside the band, unadjusted, and a case above it, adjusted).

test_nutrition.py is the one that most directly protects the "never
present partial data as complete" rule: one test computes a genuinely
high-protein result, verifies the underlying protein value really does
clear settings.high_protein_threshold_g, then confirms the tag list
comes back empty anyway once coverage is dragged down to 25% - proving
the suppression rule actually fires, not just that it's never been
triggered.

test_substitutions.py confirms flavor-impact ranking, and specifically
that a reason="dietary" call with dietary_restrictions=["dairy_free"]
excludes a dairy substitute that an otherwise-identical
reason="unavailable" call (no restriction) would happily include.

A latent test-isolation bug, found and fixed while writing these tests
------------------------------------------------------
mealsight.matching.synonyms and mealsight.matching.substitutions each
cache their loaded map process-wide, keyed by nothing - intended for a
single long-running application process, not a test suite that
constructs a fresh, different Database per test. Nothing in the test
suite had ever actually exercised load_synonym_map or
load_substitution_map against a real Database before this phase (the
matcher's own tests always pass hand-built dicts directly), so the bug
had never surfaced. test_recipe_engine/conftest.py now has an autouse
fixture that calls reset_synonym_cache() and reset_substitution_cache()
before every test, specifically so whichever test happens to run first
doesn't silently decide what every later test in the same process sees.

test_nutrition_coverage.py (in test_seed/, not test_matching or
test_recipe_engine)
------------------------------------------------------
Covered in full in section 9. Two tests, each skipping cleanly (not
failing) when its prerequisite doesn't exist on the current machine -
recipes.db for the recipe-corpus check, the vision benchmark checkpoint
file for the vision-vocabulary check.


13. DECISIONS AND ALTERNATIVES REJECTED
=============================================================

Whole-word matching versus keeping raw substring containment
    Covered in section 7. Raw substring containment is simpler code but
    genuinely wrong whenever a shorter term happens to spell out inside
    a longer, unrelated word - "egg" inside "reggiano" being the
    concrete case that actually shipped. \b-bounded regex matching costs
    almost nothing and closes the entire class of bug at once, not just
    the one instance that happened to be found.

Renaming mismatched nutrition rows versus adding duplicate new rows
    Covered in section 9. Once a canonical/nutrition-row mismatch was
    found (scallion/green onion, and the nine phase-1.4-era pairs),
    the alternative was leaving the old, unreachable row in place and
    adding a fresh row under the correct canonical name instead. That
    would have left two rows describing the same real ingredient, with
    no guarantee they'd ever be edited together again - a rename keeps
    exactly one source of truth per ingredient, matching how the
    "scallion" mistake was itself found and fixed.

Specificity matching as its own module versus folding it into normalize.py
    Cut/variety words (thigh, breast, red, baby) are deliberately never
    stripped by normalize_ingredient itself, since a recipe that
    specifically wants a boneless chicken breast is not indifferent to
    getting thighs instead for every purpose - only the matcher's own
    "is this pantry item good enough" comparison should treat them as
    interchangeable-with-a-caveat. Keeping that judgment in its own
    module (specificity.py) rather than baking it into normalization
    keeps normalize_ingredient's job single and clear: turn messy text
    into a stable string, nothing more.

Frozen vision-vocabulary snapshot versus reading the real checkpoint file
    A test that reads a small, committed, frozen list of past vision
    outputs would run identically on every machine, with no dependency
    on a gitignored artifact. This phase instead reads the real
    checkpoint file directly (skipping cleanly if it's absent), on the
    reasoning that the whole point of this check is catching a future
    vision-prompt change that introduces new vocabulary - a frozen
    snapshot can only ever check against vocabulary already known at the
    time it was frozen, and would need manual refreshing to catch
    anything new, defeating the actual purpose stated in the task this
    check was built to satisfy.

An explicit unit-to-grams table versus a per-ingredient density table
    Covered in section 10. A per-ingredient density table (a cup of
    flour weighs less than a cup of honey) would be more accurate, but
    this project has no such table, and building one accurately for
    hundreds of ingredients is its own substantial project. A single,
    documented, generic approximation is what's actually available, and
    it's labeled as approximate rather than presented as precise.

reason (a label) versus a full allergen/dietary ontology in
find_substitutions
    The task's five-tool spec only names find_substitutions(ingredient_
    name, reason) directly, but reason alone can't say which specific
    dietary restriction or allergen actually applies. Rather than
    inventing a second, parallel restriction vocabulary just for this
    one function, it reuses dietary_restrictions and the exact same tag
    set (dairy_free, nut_free, and so on) the rest of this phase already
    established - one restriction vocabulary for the whole application,
    not a different one per tool.


14. GLOSSARY
=============================================================

canonical name
    The standard, preferred name an ingredient resolves to - see phase
    1.4's notes for the fuller definition; this phase is where a
    canonical/nutrition-row mismatch first became something the code
    could actually detect.

cut/variety term
    A word denoting a specific cut, form, or variety of the same
    ingredient (thigh, breast, fillet, red, baby) - as opposed to a word
    that creates a genuinely different ingredient (sweet, cream,
    coconut). The distinction this phase's specificity matching is built
    around.

occurrence-weighted coverage
    What fraction of total ingredient *occurrences* (not just distinct
    ingredient names) have a matching reference row - see phase 1.4's
    notes for the original definition; this phase is where a second,
    separate population (vision vocabulary, not just the recipe corpus)
    started being measured this way too.

partial match
    A pantry item that's a less-specific form of what a recipe
    specifically requires (plain "chicken" against a recipe wanting
    "chicken thighs") - usable, but not confirmed to be the exact
    cut/variety wanted.

specificity match
    Either direction of the cut/variety comparison this phase's
    mealsight.matching.specificity module implements - full (pantry item
    at least as specific) or partial (pantry item less specific).

whole-word matching
    Checking whether a term appears in a string as a complete word (or
    phrase), using boundaries where the term starts and ends - as
    opposed to raw substring containment, which will also match a term
    that merely happens to spell out inside a longer, unrelated word.


15. INTERVIEW TALKING POINTS
=============================================================

Q: Walk me through what happens when a recipe needs "chicken" and the
pantry has "chicken thighs" - why did that ever fail, and how does it
work now?

    Normalizing "chicken thighs" gives you "chicken thigh" - "thigh"
    isn't a container word or a meaningless prep verb, it's a real fact
    about the cut, so the normalizer correctly leaves it in. That means
    a plain equality check against normalized "chicken" fails, and the
    original matcher reported it as fully missing, including flagging
    it critical-missing when the ingredient was importance="critical" -
    which is exactly the bug I found running the matcher against a real
    pantry. The fix was a dedicated comparison, not a normalizer change:
    compare_specificity checks whether the pantry item's extra words,
    relative to the requirement, are all recognized cut or variety
    terms - thigh, breast, fillet, and so on. If they are, the pantry
    item is at least as specific as what was asked for, so it counts as
    a full match. I kept this logic out of the normalizer specifically
    because a recipe that wants a boneless chicken breast isn't
    indifferent to getting thighs instead for every purpose - only the
    "is this good enough" comparison should treat them as
    interchangeable, with the reverse direction (recipe wants thighs,
    pantry only has plain chicken) scored as a partial match instead of
    a full one, since there's genuinely less confidence there.

Q: You found the same "raw substring instead of whole word" bug twice -
once in importance assignment, once you're flagging as still-open in
dietary tagging. Why fix one and not the other in the same session?

    Because they were found in different tasks with different explicit
    scopes, and the fix itself, while mechanically simple, changes
    output for the entire seeded recipe corpus and needs its own
    re-seed and re-verification pass every time. The importance bug -
    "egg" matching inside "reggiano" - was found and fixed as part of a
    task specifically about the importance heuristic. The dietary-tag
    version of the same bug (the same "egg" pattern would falsely match
    "eggplant") turned up while I was already in that code reasoning
    about the fix, but fixing it wasn't what was asked, and doing it
    anyway would have meant re-auditing dietary tag safety across the
    whole corpus as an unrequested side effect. I recorded it in
    docs/KNOWN_ISSUES.md instead, with exactly what a fix would involve,
    specifically so it doesn't just quietly disappear until someone
    trips over it in production the way the importance bug was tripped
    over in a demo run.

Q: The nutrition coverage figure went from a reported 100% down to 98.1%
before you brought it back up. Doesn't a coverage number getting worse
mean something regressed?

    No real data got worse - the measurement got more honest. The
    original 100% was computed by comparing raw normalized ingredient
    strings straight against raw nutrition_reference keys, with no
    synonym resolution in the loop at all. That happened to work for
    most of the recipe corpus, but it meant a nutrition row filed under
    the wrong key - "scallion" instead of "green onion", which is what
    the synonym table actually calls it - would never be caught, because
    the check never exercised the exact same synonym-resolution path a
    real nutrition lookup would use. The moment I fixed the coverage
    check to resolve through synonyms properly, nine previously-invisible
    mismatches like that surfaced at once, all left over from phase
    1.4's original synonym file choosing American canonical spellings
    that never matched the British-spelled nutrition rows underneath
    them. The dip to 98.1% was the check finally measuring the real
    thing; renaming those nine rows to their actual canonical names is
    what brought it back to a genuine 100%, not a papered-over one.

Q: calculate_nutrition converts ingredient quantities to grams using a
fixed unit table. What's the actual limitation there, and would you fix
it?

    The table treats volume units - a cup, a tablespoon - as roughly
    water density, and countable units - a clove, a can - as one rough
    average weight each. That's genuinely wrong for plenty of real
    ingredients: a cup of flour is much lighter than a cup of honey, and
    "one clove of garlic" varies a fair amount in real life. I documented
    that directly in the module rather than hiding it, and the function
    still reports ingredient coverage separately from unit-conversion
    accuracy - coverage answers "did we have nutrition data for this
    ingredient at all," not "was the gram estimate precise." I'd fix the
    density problem with a real per-ingredient density table if this
    needed to support anything closer to clinical accuracy, but building
    one correctly for hundreds of ingredients is a real, separate body of
    work, and a documented, generic approximation is an honest stand-in
    until that's actually needed.

Q: can_cook needs both a score threshold and "no critical ingredient
missing." Why not just fold the critical-missing penalty into the score
and check the score alone?

    Because the two conditions protect against different failure modes,
    and folding them into one number can hide the more dangerous one. A
    recipe missing nine unimportant ingredients out of ten unimportant
    ones can mathematically clear the same score as a recipe missing one
    single critical ingredient, depending on exactly how the arithmetic
    lines up - and I actually have a test built around exactly that:
    nine matched ingredients, one missing critical one, which nets a
    0.6 score under the formula, comfortably above the 0.5 threshold,
    and can_cook still has to come back False. If can_cook only checked
    the score, that recipe would get recommended as cookable while
    genuinely missing the one ingredient the dish doesn't work without.
    Checking both conditions means a critical gap can never be quietly
    outweighed by a pile of unimportant matches.
